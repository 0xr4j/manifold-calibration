A calibration study of prediction markets

When a market says 70%, does it happen 70% of the time?

An event contract pays $1 if something happens and $0 if it doesn't, so its price is meant to be the market's probability. This study checks that claim against thousands of resolved markets on Manifold, and lets you test the ideas yourself.

Step 1

What an event contract is

You buy a YES contract at the market price. If the event happens it pays $1; if not, it pays nothing. So if you think the true chance is higher than the price, buying YES makes money on average. Move the sliders.

That is the whole game: profit comes from the gap between the price and the true probability. If prices already equal true probabilities, nobody has an edge. Calibration measures how close they are.

Step 2 · Simulator

What calibration looks like

This simulator invents a market whose prices are off in a way you choose, rolls dice for every outcome, and draws the calibration chart a researcher would see. Points on the dashed line mean a price of p comes true p of the time.

Extremeness 1 means fair prices. Above 1, the true chance is more extreme than the price (prices are too timid). Below 1, prices are too extreme (overconfident), so long shots win more often than their price says.

Step 4

The look-ahead trap

It is tempting to measure prices "a day before resolution". But nobody knows in advance when a market will resolve, and the timing depends on the answer: questions like "will X happen by a date" resolve YES the moment X happens, while NO waits for the deadline. So any moment counted back from resolution quietly uses the outcome. A backtest built on it trades on information no trader had. That is why the headline numbers and the strategy backtest use the price 7 days after a market opened, a moment anyone could pick in advance.

Backtest a simple strategy

If prices are off in a predictable way, a trader can profit. Try two kinds of rule on the real data. Each trade buys one contract at the price 7 days after the market opened, and is settled at $1 or $0.

Read this as a lesson, not a money machine. Manifold uses play money, and buying moves an automated market maker's price against you. The price-band rule is chosen after seeing the data. The fitted rule is fairer: it learns only from markets already resolved on the split date and trades only after it. Two biases remain. Markets were picked using their final trader count, which is unknown on day 7. And a market must still be open on day 7 to be traded, which slightly favours NO (that one is fair: you would see it).

Method

How the study works

  1. Markets. Resolved YES/NO markets from the public Manifold API, each with at least 20 traders: the most popular resolved markets plus a sample of smaller recent ones. Markets resolved to a percentage or cancelled are excluded.
  2. Prices. For each market, the probability right after the last trade strictly before five moments: 7 days after it opened, halfway through its life, and 30, 7 and 1 days before it resolved. Only the first could be chosen in advance, so it is the headline. Every returned trade is checked to be earlier than the moment asked for.
  3. Backtest. The fitted rule is walk-forward: on a split date it learns only from markets already resolved, then trades only prices read after that date.
  4. Scores. Brier score mean((price − outcome)²) against a forecaster that always quotes the base rate; 95% Wilson intervals per price bin; and a calibration slope from a logistic regression of the outcome on logit(price).
  5. Limits. Manifold trades play money, creators resolve their own markets, prices are last trades rather than quotes, and the popular sample leans toward heavily traded markets. Kalshi and Polymarket were not reachable from the network this was run on.
