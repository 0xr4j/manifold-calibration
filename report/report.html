How well do prediction-market prices predict outcomes?

A calibration study of 3,915 resolved Manifold markets (created Dec 2021 – Nov 2026) · Raj Jha · code, data and interactive site: github.com/0xr4j/manifold-calibration

Seven days after opening, market prices beat always quoting the base rate by 16% on Brier score (0.202 vs 0.241, 3,290 markets). The calibration slope was 0.72 (95% CI 0.66–0.79), below 1: prices were too extreme, so long shots won more often than their prices implied and heavy favourites less often; there was a tilt toward NO: YES resolved less often than priced.

Question

An event contract's price is meant to be the probability of the event. If that holds, markets priced at 70% should resolve YES about 70% of the time. This study checks that against 3,915 resolved YES/NO markets with at least 20 traders each: the 1,915 most popular resolved markets and 2,000 smaller recent ones. For each market it reads the last traded price before fixed moments and compares it with the outcome. The headline moment is 7 days after the market opened, because it is the only one a trader could choose in advance (see "Why the moment matters").

Left: each dot is a price bin, with the share of its markets that resolved YES and a 95% Wilson interval; the histogram shows where prices sat. Right: every market folded onto its favourite. A dot below zero means favourites at that price won less often than their price said.

Prices look far more accurate close to resolution, but every moment except the first is counted back from the resolution time, which no trader knows in advance. That timing also depends on the outcome: 64% of YES markets were resolved while trading was still open, against 30% of NO markets, because "will X happen by a date" resolves YES as soon as X happens and NO only at the deadline. A day before resolution, markets that ran to their deadline scored a Brier of 0.068 (with a day left, the answer was usually settled; only 34% of these prices were read after trading had closed), while markets resolved while still open scored 0.106, often because the event itself was a surprise. Late accuracy therefore says as much about how a question resolves as about forecasting skill, and a backtest keyed to it would trade on information it could not have had.

Manifold moves a market's close time to the moment it resolves when an open market is resolved, so a close within a minute of resolution marks a market resolved while trading was still open. This includes early resolutions and some resolved on their deadline day before trading closed.

Seven days after opening, markets with the most traders were less accurate than markets with the fewest (Brier 0.233 vs 0.174; slope 0.50 vs 0.94). More traders did not mean better prices here. The most-traded third comes mostly from the popular sample, which spans more years and attracts long-running, contested questions, so trader count, question type and time period are confounded; this is a description, not evidence that liquidity hurts accuracy.

What this means for pricing

  • The market price is a usable prior, not an answer. A week in, prices removed 16% of the base rate's Brier error. A pricing system can start from the market and look for systematic adjustments.
  • A real mispricing is not yet a tradable edge. A calibration slope of 0.72 suggests a one-line re-mapping, fair probability = sigmoid(a + b × logit(price)). Fitted only on the 1,459 markets resolved before Sep 2025 and applied to the 1,645 prices read after it (buying whichever side the correction favoured by at least 3%), it made +0.9¢ per contract over 1,390 trades (95% CI -1.2¢ to +3.1¢), before fees. That is not distinguishable from zero, and about the size of a typical 1¢ fee. The slope learned before Sep 2025 was 0.55; on the later markets it was 0.89. The mispricing was real, but its size shifted between periods, so a pricing model has to keep re-learning it rather than fix it once.
  • Only test on moments a trader could have chosen. Horizons counted back from resolution leak the outcome through its timing. The interactive site's backtest trades only at 7 days after opening and learns its correction only from markets already resolved.

Limits

  • Manifold uses play money (mana), so incentives are weaker than on Kalshi or Polymarket, which were not reachable from the network this was run on. All 3,915 markets traded in mana.
  • Markets were selected using their final popularity and trader count, which are not known 7 days in. A market must still be open 7 days in to have a price then (625 resolved sooner and are excluded at that moment). Creators resolve their own markets.
  • Prices are last trades, not quotes. A market is left out of a moment that came before it opened or before its first trade: 1 halfway, 1,581 at 30 days before the end (mostly markets that lived less than 30 days), 631 at 7 days and 4 at 1 day.

Method: Brier score and log loss against a base-rate forecaster; Wilson intervals per price bin; calibration slope and intercept from a logistic regression of the outcome on logit(price), with 1,000 bootstrap resamples of markets; Murphy decomposition (7 days after opening: reliability 0.0053, resolution 0.0435, uncertainty 0.2412). Code: fetch.py, metrics.py, analyze.py, report.py, with tests on synthetic data. Every number here is generated from the data.
